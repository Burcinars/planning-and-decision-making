The Map and the Mind

Planning and decision making, told through a company that mortgaged its own logo and a coffee chain that had to tear up its plan.

Every organization runs on two things: a map, the plans and goals that say where it is going, and a mind, the human judgment that draws the map and makes every call along the way. This unit is about both, and about the gap between them.

Act One · Part 1 · The $17 Billion Hole

September 2006: the worst forecast in Ford's history

Alan Mulally's first week as Ford's CEO began with a briefing no one would want. The internal forecast said Ford would lose $17 billion that year. The year ultimately closed at a $12.7 billion loss, the worst in the company's 103 year history, but the forecast is what Mulally walked into: a century old icon, bleeding out.

$17Bprojected loss for 2006, the forecast on Mulally's desk
$12.7Bthe actual 2006 loss, the worst in Ford history
$23.6Bborrowed by mortgaging nearly everything Ford owned

To fund a rescue, Ford borrowed $23.6 billion, pledging its factories, its headquarters, and even the Blue Oval logo itself as collateral. Read that again: the company pawned its own name. If the plan failed, Ford would not just go bankrupt. It would lose the right to its own badge.

Hold this picture. The whole of Act One explains how the red bars became green.

Here is the strange part. What saved Ford was not a revolutionary car, a genius acquisition, or a lucky break. By the time Mulally retired in 2014, Ford had posted 19 consecutive profitable quarters, and the two tools he used most were a small laminated card and a Thursday morning meeting.

A card and a meeting. That is what a plan looks like when it works. Let's look at the card first.

Act One · Part 1 · The $17 Billion Hole

The card in every pocket

Mulally condensed the entire rescue into a wallet sized card called One Ford and handed it to every employee. One side carried the plan. The other side carried the behaviors expected of everyone, from the factory floor to the boardroom.

* Yes, "own working together" reads like a typo. It is not. That is the card's exact wording, and Ford people can still recite it.

Now notice what that little card contains, because it is secretly a complete vocabulary lesson. Management distinguishes five layers, from the most timeless to the most concrete:

Mission · why we exist

The organization's purpose, the reason it deserves to exist at all.

Vision · where we are going

The future the organization is trying to create. Ford's: "profitable growth for all."

Goals · what we must achieve

Broad outcomes that would make the vision real, like "return to profitability."

Objectives · the same, with numbers and dates

Specific, measurable targets with deadlines. The ruler you can hold against reality.

Plans · how we will do it

The concrete actions, resources, and timelines that connect today to the target.

One word, two jobs

Before you climb, be honest about the word plan, because it does double duty. In everyday speech, "the plan," like the One Ford plan itself, means the whole package: vision, goals, objectives, and actions bound into one document. On the ladder, "plans" names just the bottom rung: the actions, resources, and timelines, the how.

Both uses are correct. The ladder exists so that when you unpack any plan document, you can still tell its targets, outcomes you can check, from its how, the work that chases them. A target with no actions is a wish. Actions with no target are just motion. A real plan needs both, which is exactly why the one word ends up covering both.

Five words, one ladder, from why down to how. On the next page, you get to climb it yourself.

Act One · Part 2 · The Cascade

"You sold seven million of them. Why did you stop?"

A few weeks into the job, Mulally asked his team a simple question: what happened to the Taurus? It had been one of the best selling cars in America, nearly seven million sold. The answer: it had been quietly discontinued.

Mulally's response became Ford legend. Bring it back. How long to make a new one? About four years, the engineers said. "Do it in two."

Now freeze that moment, because you just watched a plan change altitude. Mulally's survival vision ("profitable growth for all") is useless to an engineer on a Tuesday. "A new Taurus in two years" is something a factory can act on. Management gives the altitudes names:

Each level translates the one above it into something more concrete. That translation is called the goal cascade.

When the cascade works, a factory worker tightening bolts can trace a line from today's shift target all the way up to "profitable growth for all." When it breaks, strategy lives in slide decks and daily work drifts wherever habit takes it.

Act One · Part 2 · The Cascade

Plans you write once, plans you keep forever

Goals come in altitudes. Plans come in lifespans. Walk into any Starbucks during the pandemic spring of 2020 and you could see both kinds operating at once.

Single use plans · run once, then retire

Programs and projects built for one occasion. Converting up to 400 stores to pickup formats was a program. This one store's drive thru retrofit, with its own budget and end date, was a project. When it is done, the plan is done.

Standing plans · run every day, indefinitely

Policies set guardrails for judgment ("prioritize partner safety"). Procedures script a sequence (the exact steps of a curbside handoff). Rules allow no judgment at all ("no lobby seating during the outbreak"). They fire automatically, thousands of times a day.

Why bother with the distinction? Because standing plans are how organizations make routine decisions without meetings. Every procedure is a decision somebody made once, carefully, so that thousands of people never have to make it again. Keep that sentence. It comes back in Act Two wearing a different name.

Also in the toolkit · the contingency plan

A plan for if, not for when: pre-decided responses to scenarios you hope never arrive. Airlines rehearse engine failures. Good companies sketch "what if demand drops 30%?" before it does. Hold this idea. Part 5 shows what happens to a company that suddenly needs one.

Act One · Part 3 · SMART

June 2020: the announcement that did not hedge

Spring 2020. Lockdowns emptied the cafés, and roughly 80% of Starbucks orders were already to go before the pandemic even hit. Everyone knew stores had to change. Watch the difference between what Starbucks could have said and what it actually said in June 2020:

What most companies say

"We will adapt our store formats to meet customers where they are in this new environment."

What Starbucks said

"Over the next 18 months, we will close up to 400 company operated stores in the Americas and accelerate new pickup and drive thru formats."

Read both out loud. The first one cannot fail, which is exactly what is wrong with it: nothing that can never fail can ever be checked, funded, or finished. The second one has a number, a scope, and a clock. Real estate teams could act on it the same afternoon.

There is a five letter checklist hiding inside that difference, and decades of research behind why it works. Next page.

Act One · Part 3 · SMART

SMART, and the science underneath it

The checklist is called SMART, first published by consultant George Doran in 1981, and it is the most used goal tool in management for a reason:

SMART is the checklist. The science is goal setting theory, built over decades by psychologists Edwin Locke and Gary Latham. Across hundreds of studies, the same result keeps landing:

Locke & Latham's finding

Specific, challenging goals reliably produce higher performance than vague goals or "do your best."

"Do your best" sounds motivating and measures nothing, so effort quietly negotiates itself downward. A specific, hard target does two jobs at once: it directs attention to what matters and it calibrates effort to the gap.

One caution before you fall in love: a goal can pass all five letters and still be the wrong goal, aimed at the wrong thing entirely. SMART checks a goal's construction, not its wisdom. Whether the target itself is wise is a judgment call, and judgment is Act Two's whole subject.

Now let's stress test the checklist on a real goal, one letter at a time.

Act One · Part 4 · The Thursday Room

Thursday, 7 AM, Dearborn

The second tool of the turnaround was a meeting. Every Thursday at 7 AM, Mulally's entire leadership team met for the Business Plan Review, the BPR. No delegates, no excuses, no side deals before or after. The walls held hundreds of charts, every piece of the One Ford plan, each chart owned by a named executive, color coded like a traffic light.

The rhythm mattered as much as the room: same meeting, same charts, every single week. Nobody could hide a problem for long, and nobody had to guess how the plan was going. Mulally had a phrase for it: "The data sets you free."

But in his first weeks, the room had a problem. Ford was heading toward a multibillion dollar loss... and every single chart on the wall was green. Mulally finally said it out loud: we are losing billions this year. Is there nothing that is not going well?

What happened next became the most famous moment of the turnaround.

Act One · Part 4 · The Thursday Room

The first red chart

A few weeks in, executive Mark Fields had a real problem: a defect was going to delay the launch of the new Edge. Showing that meant walking into the Thursday room with the first red chart Ford's leadership had seen in years. People at Ford had been fired for less. He showed it anyway.

Silence. And then Mulally started to applaud. "Great visibility, Mark. Who can help him with this?" Within minutes, other executives were offering engineers and ideas. Within weeks, the wall of fake green had bloomed into honest reds and yellows, and problems started getting solved in the open.

What you are looking at is not a feel-good story. It is a management system with a name and a lineage:

MBO · management by objectives

Proposed by Peter Drucker in the 1950s: cascade objectives down the organization, agree on them with each owner, then review results against them on a regular rhythm. The BPR is MBO running at full power: named owners, cascaded targets, a weekly heartbeat.

OKRs · the modern descendant

At your first job you will likely meet OKRs: Objectives and Key Results, the format that traveled from Intel to Google and onward. An inspiring objective, three to five measurable key results, reviewed quarterly. Different acronym, same DNA: goals mean nothing without a review rhythm attached.

Drucker's MBO and today's OKRs both run this same loop. The magic ingredient is step 3 actually happening.

And the applause? That was Mulally protecting the one resource the whole system runs on: the truth. On the next page, you get to sit in his chair and find out how easy that is to destroy.

Researchers call that safety psychological safety, and it is the quiet ingredient inside every accountability system that actually works. Accountability without safety produces beautifully green, completely fictional charts.

Act One · Part 5 · Plans Meet the World

The "if" nobody planned for

Ford shows what planning looks like when it works. Starbucks is the harder, more honest lesson: what planning looks like when the world refuses to cooperate.

Spring 2020. Lockdowns empty every café on earth. Starbucks had spent fifty years building its identity as the "third place", the spot between home and work where people linger. Suddenly lingering was illegal.

Remember the contingency plan from Part 2, the plan for if? Starbucks had one advantage: its stores in China had hit lockdowns weeks before the West, so the company had already rehearsed safety protocols, app based ordering surges, and store closures on one continent before needing them on another. When the wave reached the Americas, the response was fast and specific: the June 2020 program you met in Part 3, up to 400 store closures in 18 months, replaced by pickup counters and drive thru lanes, riding the fact that about 80% of orders were already to go.

This is adaptive planning: sensing that the assumptions under a plan have died, and replanning fast instead of defending the old map. By 2023, sales had climbed back past pre pandemic levels, and the story could have ended there, as a clean triumph.

It did not end there.

Act One · Part 5 · Plans Meet the World

A plan is a hypothesis

So was the Triple Shot a bad plan? Careful. It was specific, measurable, ambitious, aligned, everything Act One teaches. What it turned out to be was a falsified hypothesis: a set of claims about the future that the future then voted against.

The deepest idea in Act One

A plan is a hypothesis, not a promise. Setting goals is proposing a theory about the future. Reviewing them is running the experiment. Revising them is not failure. It is the accountability.

Look at both companies through that lens and they merge into one lesson. Ford's Thursday room worked because it treated the plan as revisable: every week, reality got to talk back, and the plan changed shape hundreds of times on its way to those 19 profitable quarters. Starbucks' reset was painful partly because the talking back happened at CEO scale instead of chart scale.

One question is left hanging, and Act One cannot answer it. The Triple Shot's authors were brilliant, experienced, and drowning in data. Why did they believe targets that would not survive two years? For that, we have to leave the map and open up the planner.

🧪 Experiment 6 · The estimate

The opera house problem

Sydney, 1957. A young Danish architect wins a competition to build an opera house on the harbor. The official plan: four years and 7 million Australian dollars. The sail shaped design will become one of the most recognizable buildings on earth.

You know how this genre of story goes, so let's make it precise. The estimate was A$7 million. Drag to your guess of the final cost, then lock it in.

A$7M · the estimateA$140M
A$7Myour guess
A$102Mthe actual cost · about 14× the estimate
Slide, then lock it in.
The planning fallacy

The Opera House opened in 1973, a decade late, at A$102 million, roughly fourteen times the estimate. Psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky named the pattern the planning fallacy: humans systematically underestimate the time, cost, and risk of their own plans, even when they know that similar plans have run over before.

Researcher Bent Flyvbjerg, who studies megaprojects for a living, finds that roughly nine out of ten run over budget. The Opera House is not an outlier. It is the club mascot.

And here is the bridge you are standing on. The fallacy is not a planning error, it is a thinking error that lives inside planners. Every plan in Act One, the brilliant ones and the doomed ones, was produced by the three pound organ this next act is about. Welcome to the mind.

Act Two · Part 7 · The Ideal and the Real

The spreadsheet in your head

You have already used the most famous model in decision science, probably during apartment season. You made a spreadsheet, or at least a mental one: rent, commute, roommates, natural light. You decided rent mattered most, gave every option a score, and picked the winner.

Congratulations: that is the rational decision making model, the textbook ideal of how choices should be made. Its full sequence:

Does deciding like this actually pay? The evidence says yes: in a PwC survey of more than 1,000 senior executives, highly data driven organizations were three times more likely to report significant improvements in decision making than companies that lean on gut alone.

And the stakes are enormous, because deciding is most of what managers do. Act Two · Part 7 · The Ideal and the Real

It is 9 PM and you are hungry

Nobody builds a weighted spreadsheet at 9 PM on an empty stomach. You scroll a few minutes, find a place that is good enough, and order. You did not review every restaurant in town. You did not optimize. You stopped at acceptable, and it was the right call.

Economist Herbert Simon won a Nobel Prize for taking that move seriously. He called it satisficing, a blend of satisfy and suffice: searching until you find an option that clears your bar, then stopping. And he explained why we do it with an idea called bounded rationality: real decision makers are hemmed in by three walls.

Bounded rationality · Herbert Simon

We are rational, but only within limits: we never have all the information, all the processing power, or all the time. So instead of the best option, we pick the best option we can find at reasonable cost.

Here is the part students miss: satisficing is not the lazy cousin of the rational model. Given real constraints, it is often the smarter strategy, because search itself costs time and money. A hiring manager who interviews until a candidate clears the bar, then stops, is not cutting corners. She is spending the company's attention wisely.

The skill is knowing when you are doing which. Low stakes, reversible, time pressed: satisfice without guilt. High stakes, irreversible, rich data: slow down and climb the seven steps. Which raises a beautiful question: can a manager sort decisions in advance, so each one gets the right treatment? They can, and you already learned half the trick in Act One.

Act Two · Part 7 · The Ideal and the Real

The terrain of decisions

In Part 2 you met standing plans: policies, procedures, rules. Here is that idea wearing its decision science name. A programmed decision is one that recurs so predictably that the organization has decided it once, carefully, in advance, and turned it into a rule. A barista does not convene a meeting when the oat milk runs low. Stock below six cartons: reorder. The decision was made years ago and simply fires.

A nonprogrammed decision is the opposite: novel, consequential, no script available. Which 400 stores close? Nobody has a procedure for that. Somebody has to actually decide.

Cutting across that pair is a second question: how much do you know about the outcomes?

Put the two ideas together and you get the terrain map. Down in the flatlands, programmed decisions under certainty run on autopilot, and should: every good procedure is a decision made once so a thousand people never make it again. Up in the hills, nonprogrammed decisions under uncertainty are where managers earn their pay, and where every tool in the rest of this unit lives.

Time to walk the terrain yourself.

Act Two · Part 8 · The Gallery

Shortcuts, and their shadows

Somewhere on a wine list tonight, thousands of people will use the same rule: "never the cheapest, never the most expensive, something in the middle." No spreadsheet, no seven steps, decision made in four seconds.

That rule is a heuristic: a mental shortcut that trades a little accuracy for a lot of speed. And before we spend six pages on how shortcuts fail, be fair to them: they mostly work. Psychologist Gerd Gigerenzer calls well trained gut feel "unconscious intelligence": an ER doctor or a fire captain deciding in seconds is running compressed experience, and often beats slow analysis under pressure.

But Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky spent their careers documenting the shadow side: shortcuts fail in systematic, predictable ways. Not random mistakes. The same mistakes, by everyone, in the same situations. Those recurring failure patterns are called cognitive biases, and a manager who can name them can catch them.

The gallery ahead

Six biases, chosen because they cost organizations the most: anchoring, confirmation, groupthink, loss aversion, sunk cost, availability. One story each. You have already met a seventh, the planning fallacy, out on the bridge.

First, though, an experiment. Because the strangest thing about biases is that they work on you even while you are studying them, let's catch one in the act. You will need to make two small purchasing decisions. Next page.

Act Two · Part 8 · The Gallery · Bias 1

Anchoring

A hiring manager asks a candidate about salary expectations. The candidate says "$100,000." Market data says the role pays $80,000. Watch what happens: the negotiation now orbits the first number, and the final offer lands closer to it than the data justifies. The candidate did not persuade anyone. She just spoke first.

Anchoring bias

The first number on the table drags every later judgment toward itself, even when it is arbitrary.

Experiments show the pull works even with numbers people know are random, and retailers weaponize it daily: "Original price $200, now $150" makes $150 feel like a win regardless of what the item is worth. In business it anchors forecasts too: last year's revenue target quietly becomes the starting point for next year's, whether or not the world still supports it.

The counter: know your numbers before hearing anyone else's, and when you can, speak first. Against a set anchor, deliberately argue the opposite reference point out loud.

Act Two · Part 8 · The Gallery · Bias 2

Confirmation bias

A manager is convinced a new technology will transform productivity. Watch how he "researches" it: case studies where it worked get forwarded to the team. Reports where it flopped are dismissed as badly run rollouts. As McKinsey describes the move: "I have a hunch that investing in X would create value, so let's look for supporting facts." The conclusion came first. The evidence is decoration.

Confirmation bias

We hunt for evidence that agrees with what we already believe, and grade contradicting evidence on a much harsher curve.

This one is dangerous precisely because it feels like diligence: the manager above genuinely believes he did his homework. It skews business cases, pilot evaluations, and hiring debriefs, anywhere a human with a favorite hypothesis gets to choose what counts.

The counter has a beautiful one line version: try to prove yourself wrong. Assign someone to argue the opposite. Ask "what evidence would change my mind?" before gathering any. Part 9 turns this into a formal tool.

Act Two · Part 8 · The Gallery · Bias 3

Groupthink

April 1961. President Kennedy's advisory team, one of the most credentialed rooms ever assembled, approves the invasion of Cuba at the Bay of Pigs. It fails catastrophically within three days. Afterward, several advisors admitted the same thing: they had doubts and said nothing. The room felt unanimous, so each doubter concluded he must be the only one.

Groupthink · named by Irving Janis, 1972

When a group prizes harmony over accuracy, dissent gets self censored, and the room becomes more confident and less correct at the same time.

Be precise about the lesson, because it is not "groups decide badly." Groups beat individuals when they truly pool information: more perspectives, more error catching, more buy in. Groupthink is the failure mode where pooling stops, and it has process cures: a rotating devil's advocate, the nominal group technique (everyone writes ideas silently before any discussion), and Delphi rounds (anonymous expert input). The common thread: collect judgments independently, then combine them.

Act Two · Part 8 · The Gallery · Bias 4

Loss aversion

Offer a manager a project with a 70% chance of gaining $10 million and a 30% chance of losing $3 million, and watch many of them pass. Run the numbers: the expected value is strongly positive. But the imagined loss feels bigger than the imagined gain, and the feeling wins the meeting.

Loss aversion · Kahneman & Tversky's prospect theory

Losses hurt roughly twice as much as equal gains please. So we overpay to avoid losses, and walk past good bets.

It follows that framing changes decisions: "90% chance of success" and "10% chance of failure" describe the same project, and reliably get different votes. And at company scale, loss aversion compounds into stagnation: every individual bet gets rejected for its downside, and the portfolio quietly starves.

The counter: judge bets as a portfolio, not one by one, and reframe the ledger to include what inaction loses. Jeff Bezos built this into Amazon's culture by splitting failure in two: experimental failures (good, that is what bets cost) versus operational failures (bad execution). Only one of them should be punished.

Act Two · Part 8 · The Gallery · Bias 5

Sunk cost, and the courage to stop

The bias is named after an airplane. British and French governments knew for years that the Concorde would never earn back its development costs. They kept funding it anyway, for decades, because so much had already been spent. Economists now literally call it the Concorde fallacy.

Sunk cost fallacy · escalation of commitment

Letting money already spent, which no decision can recover, drag you into spending more. Rationally, only future costs and benefits should vote.

You run this bias at movie night: "it's terrible, but we've watched an hour, let's finish it." Organizations run it with millions: the project is failing, but killing it means admitting the spend, so it survives another quarter, and another.

The counterexample is instructive. Quibi, the streaming app that raised $1.75 billion, launched into the pandemic and flopped instantly. Its founders shut it down within six months and returned remaining cash to investors rather than burning it on hope. Brutal, and rational. The counter in ordinary companies: set kill criteria in advance ("if metrics are below X by Q2, we stop") and put someone who was not part of the original decision on the review, because they are not defending their own past.

Act Two · Part 8 · The Gallery · Bias 6

Availability

After news coverage of a plane crash, people drive instead of fly, though driving is far more dangerous per mile. The crash is vivid and recent, so it feels probable. The car risk is statistical wallpaper, so it feels like nothing.

Availability bias

Whatever comes to mind easily gets judged as common and important. Memorability masquerades as probability.

In organizations, availability sets agendas. A competitor's flashy AI demo dominates three strategy meetings while a slow, unglamorous rise in customer churn, the genuinely bigger threat, gets a slide nobody reads. Last quarter's loud crisis hogs this quarter's budget.

The counter: ask for the base rate, the boring long run data, before reacting to the vivid case. "How often does this actually happen?" is one of the cheapest, most powerful questions in management. Keep it loaded.

Act Two · Part 9 · The Fixes

Systems beat willpower

Daniel Kahneman (1934 to 2024) spent fifty years cataloguing the biases you just toured, and when asked near the end of his life whether there was hope for us, his answer was surprisingly cheerful, just not about individuals. He was far more optimistic about organizations, he said, because organizations can do the thing brains cannot: put systems in place.

You have already seen one: Ford's Thursday room is a debiasing machine wearing a business suit. Here are five more, each aimed at biases you can now name:

The premortem · invented by psychologist Gary Klein. Before committing, the team imagines it is one year later and the project has failed. Everyone writes down why. Doubt becomes an assignment instead of a betrayal. Counters: overconfidence, planning fallacy, groupthink.

The outside view · from Kahneman and Lovallo, industrialized by Flyvbjerg as reference class forecasting. Forecast from the track record of similar projects, not from your project's inner details. "How long did the last ten take?" beats "how long does this one feel like?" Counters: planning fallacy, availability.

Assigned dissent · a rotating devil's advocate or a full red team whose job is to attack the plan. Intel's Andy Grove built a culture of "constructive confrontation" on exactly this. Dissent by role, so no one has to be brave. Counters: groupthink, confirmation.

Decision checklists · pilots do not trust memory at 500 miles per hour, and big decisions deserve the same humility: Did we consider three real alternatives? What evidence would change our mind? Who disagrees, and did we hear them? Counters: skipped steps under pressure, confirmation.

Choice architecture · Thaler and Sunstein's nudges: redesign the default so the better decision happens more often. Require every hiring slate to include nontraditional candidates. Require an independent reviewer before any troubled project gets new funding. Counters: habit, implicit bias, sunk cost.

Notice the shared design: none of these ask anyone to be less biased. They change the process so the bias has nowhere to hide. Time to prescribe them yourself.

Act Two · Part 9 · The Fixes

⚠️ When the evidence itself lies

Remember the transparency note at the start, the famous study this unit had to remove? Here is that story, told properly, because it belongs in this unit more than the finding ever did.

For about a decade, business courses everywhere, including the earlier version of this unit, taught a lovely finding from a 2012 paper: make people sign an honesty pledge at the top of a form instead of the bottom, and they report more truthfully. It had everything: famous authors, including Dan Ariely of the decoy study, a real insurance company field experiment, and an irresistibly practical moral.

How it unraveled

In 2020, a much larger replication attempt found no effect at all. Then, in 2021, data sleuths at the blog Data Colada examined the original insurance data and showed it could not be real: the numbers had patterns real customers do not produce. The paper was retracted. The fallout kept growing: Harvard later found research misconduct in separate studies by coauthor Francesca Gino and, in 2025, took the nearly unprecedented step of revoking her tenure, which she continues to contest. Duke investigated Ariely, who denies fabricating the data. Who faked those numbers is still disputed. That the finding is dead is not.

Why put this in a management unit? Three reasons, and they are all things you now have names for.

First: notice why everyone believed it. The finding was tidy, useful, and charming, which meant thousands of smart people, professors included, wanted it to be true and stopped checking. That is confirmation bias, operating on the consumers of evidence, not just its producers.

Second: notice who caught it. Researchers doing exactly what Part 9 prescribes: trying to prove an attractive claim wrong. The premortem mindset, aimed at evidence.

Third: calibrate, don't despair. The decoy study you ran earlier has been replicated many times, which is why it is still here. The lesson is not "trust no research." It is a manager's checklist for evidence: Has it replicated? Who benefits if I believe it? Is it suspiciously convenient? One tidy study should start an investigation, never end one.

Act Two · Part 9 · The Fixes

One more decision maker just joined the room

You will manage alongside AI, so this unit owes you the newest finding in it. In 2025, Harvard Business Review reported an experiment with hundreds of executives forecasting a stock price. Half got help from a generative AI assistant. The result: the AI assisted group made worse predictions, and felt more confident making them.

The mechanism should feel familiar by now. A fluent, confident sounding answer becomes an anchor. Its polish invites automation bias, the tendency to trust the machine's output over your own checking. The executives were not replaced by AI. They were amplified by it, overconfidence included.

The rule for the AI decade

AI is an input to judgment, not a substitute for it. Every bias in the gallery applies with full force to a human reading a model's output.

Practical version: ask AI for the case against its own answer, check its claims the way you now check any evidence, and keep the decision's name, yours, attached to it. This unit was built that way: AI assisted, human checked, professor accountable.
